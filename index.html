A Quiet Study · For a Friend

~ Shadows & Echoes ~ The Promised One in the
Hebrew Scriptures

A reference companion — the death and rising of Jesus, and the way the older books seem to whisper of him long before he came.

This is meant simply — as a study sheet. Not an argument, not a debate. Just the verses themselves, laid side by side, so you can read them slowly and weigh them honestly.

You and I share a deep reverence for Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets. What follows are passages from their books — books your tradition also honors as sent down — alongside what the Christian scriptures say happened in Jesus. Read them as a friend who is curious. Nothing more is asked.

I · The Account

What the Gospels say happened

Before looking at the shadows, here is the substance — the death and resurrection of Jesus as the Christian scriptures record them. The verses below are the heart of the claim.

1 Corinthians 15:3–4
For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that the Messiah died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures.
John 19:30
When he had received the drink, Jesus said, "It is finished." With that, he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.
Luke 24:5–7
"Why do you look for the living among the dead? He is not here; he has risen! Remember how he told you, while he was still with you in Galilee: 'The Son of Man must be delivered over to the hands of sinners, be crucified and on the third day be raised again.'"
Romans 4:25
He was delivered over to death for our sins and was raised to life for our justification.
1 Peter 2:24
He himself bore our sins in his body on the cross, so that we might die to sins and live for righteousness; by his wounds you have been healed.
II · The Meaning

What the atonement is

In the Hebrew Bible, sin is taken seriously — it separates a person from God, and a price is paid to cover it. The word atonement means "to cover" or "to reconcile." Christians believe Jesus willingly bore that cost on behalf of those who could not bear it themselves — not because God lacked mercy, but because his mercy and his justice met perfectly in one act of self-giving love.

i.

Substitution

One stands in the place of another. The innocent bears what the guilty owed — a pattern visible in every sacrifice from Eden onward.

ii.

Covering

Sin is not ignored, but covered — the way Adam and Eve were clothed by God himself with skins from a slain animal.

iii.

Reconciliation

Distance becomes nearness. What was broken between humanity and God is mended through one perfect mediator.

iv.

Victory

Death itself is swallowed up. The resurrection is God's public declaration that the offering was accepted and the curse undone.

III · The Foreshadowings

Shadows of the cross in the older books

Christians read the Hebrew Scriptures and see a pattern: the same story sketched again and again in different keys, until the One it pointed to finally arrived. Here are the most striking — read each Hebrew passage on its own first, then compare.

i.
Abraham & the Beloved Son
Genesis 22

A father takes his only beloved son to a mountain — Moriah, the very place where Jerusalem would later stand. The son carries the wood of his own sacrifice up the hill. At the last moment, a ram caught in a thicket dies in the son's place, and Abraham names the place The Lord Will Provide.

In Genesis
  • "Take your son, your only son, whom you love" (Gen 22:2)
  • Isaac carries the wood up the mountain (Gen 22:6)
  • "God himself will provide the lamb" (Gen 22:8)
  • A ram dies in place of the son (Gen 22:13)
  • Mount Moriah — same region as Jerusalem (Gen 22:2; 2 Chr 3:1)
In the Gospels
  • "This is my Son, whom I love" (Matt 3:17)
  • Jesus carries his cross up the hill (John 19:17)
  • John calls him "the Lamb of God" (John 1:29)
  • The Son dies; we go free (Rom 8:32)
  • Crucified just outside Jerusalem
Worth pausing on Romans 8:32 — "He who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all..." The wording deliberately echoes Genesis 22:12, where God says to Abraham, "you have not withheld your son." What Abraham was spared from doing, Christians say God himself did.
ii.
Joseph — the Rejected Brother
Genesis 37–50

Joseph (Yusuf) is one of the most beloved figures in both our traditions. The parallels with Jesus are remarkable — and the moment he reveals himself to his brothers is one of the most moving scenes in all of scripture.

In Joseph's Life
  • The beloved son of his father
  • Rejected and betrayed by his own brothers
  • Sold for pieces of silver (Gen 37:28)
  • Falsely accused, condemned though innocent
  • Counted among criminals in prison
  • Raised up to the right hand of Pharaoh
  • Becomes the only source of bread that saves the world from death (Gen 41:57)
  • His brothers don't recognize him when they meet him
In Jesus' Life
  • "This is my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11)
  • Rejected by his own people (John 1:11)
  • Betrayed for thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:15)
  • Falsely accused, condemned though innocent
  • Crucified between two criminals (Luke 23:33)
  • Raised up to the right hand of God (Acts 2:33)
  • "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35)
  • Disciples on the Emmaus road don't recognize him (Luke 24:16)
The reveal — Genesis 45 & Isaiah 53 Read Joseph's words to his terrified brothers: "I am Joseph your brother, whom you sold... do not be distressed... God sent me ahead of you to save lives" (Gen 45:4–5). Then read Isaiah 53 — written centuries later — where the suffering servant is "despised and rejected," "wounded for our transgressions," and yet turns out to be the one through whom God saves his people. The pattern is the same: the one we wronged is the very one who saves us. Christians believe this is what happens at the cross.
iii.
The Passover Lamb
Exodus 12

On the night Israel was rescued from Egypt, every household took a lamb — without blemish, a male in its first year — slaughtered it, and painted its blood on the doorposts. When death passed through the land, it passed over every house marked by the blood. The lamb died so the firstborn would live.

The Passover Lamb
  • A lamb without defect (Ex 12:5)
  • "Not one of its bones shall be broken" (Ex 12:46)
  • Selected on the 10th, killed on the 14th of Nisan
  • Blood on the wood of the doorposts
  • Death passes over those covered by the blood
  • Eaten — the people take the lamb into themselves
Jesus at Passover
  • "A lamb without blemish or defect" (1 Pet 1:19)
  • "Not one of his bones will be broken" (John 19:36)
  • Enters Jerusalem on the 10th, dies on the 14th — Passover
  • His blood on the wood of the cross
  • "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Cor 5:7)
  • "This is my body... given for you" (Luke 22:19)
John the Baptist's word John 1:29 — "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" A first-century Jew, steeped in the Passover, hearing those words on the eve of Passover season — would have understood exactly what was being claimed.
iv.
The Serpent Lifted Up
Numbers 21:4–9

In the wilderness, the people are bitten by venomous snakes and dying. God tells Moses to make a bronze serpent and lift it up on a pole. Anyone bitten — anyone at all — only has to look at it and they will live. Salvation by sight. By trust.

In the Wilderness
  • The people are dying from a deadly bite
  • Moses lifts up the bronze serpent on a pole
  • The image of the curse becomes the means of healing
  • Whoever looks in faith lives — no other requirement
Jesus' Own Words
  • Humanity dying in sin (Rom 6:23)
  • Jesus lifted up on the cross
  • "Becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13)
  • "Whoever believes may have eternal life in him" (John 3:15)
Jesus draws the line himself — John 3:14–15 "Just as Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be lifted up, that everyone who believes may have eternal life in him." Jesus is the one who tells us to read Numbers 21 this way. Worth weighing carefully.
v.
Psalm 22 — David's Strange Song
Psalm 22 (c. 1000 BC)

David writes a psalm about his own suffering — but it describes things that never happened to David. Written roughly a thousand years before crucifixion was even invented as a Roman execution method, it reads like an eyewitness account of one. Read it slowly and on its own.

Psalm 22 (1000 BC)
  • v.1 — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"
  • v.7 — "All who see me mock me; they hurl insults, shaking their heads"
  • v.8 — "He trusts in the Lord; let the Lord rescue him"
  • v.14 — "All my bones are out of joint... my heart has melted"
  • v.15 — "My tongue sticks to the roof of my mouth"
  • v.16 — "They pierce my hands and my feet"
  • v.18 — "They divide my clothes among them and cast lots for my garment"
  • v.31 — ends with "He has done it" — finished, accomplished
At the Crucifixion
  • Jesus quotes this exact verse from the cross (Matt 27:46)
  • "Those who passed by hurled insults, shaking their heads" (Matt 27:39)
  • Mocked with these very words (Matt 27:43)
  • The physical effects of crucifixion — dehydration, dislocation
  • "I am thirsty" (John 19:28)
  • His hands and feet pierced by Roman nails
  • Soldiers cast lots for his clothing (John 19:23–24)
  • His final word: "It is finished" (John 19:30)
Read it cold, then read Matthew 27 The most honest thing to do is to read Psalm 22 entirely on its own — without any commentary — then read the crucifixion accounts in Matthew 27 and John 19. The piercing of hands and feet is especially striking: the psalm was written long before crucifixion existed as a form of execution.
vi.
Isaiah 53 — The Suffering Servant
Isaiah 52:13 – 53:12 (c. 700 BC)

This is perhaps the most extraordinary passage in the Hebrew Bible. Written some seven hundred years before Jesus, it describes a servant of God who suffers and dies for the sins of others, and is then mysteriously vindicated. Read it whole, in one sitting, before reading anything else about it.

Isaiah's Servant (700 BC)
  • 53:3 — "Despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows"
  • 53:5 — "Pierced for our transgressions, crushed for our iniquities"
  • 53:5 — "By his wounds we are healed"
  • 53:6 — "The Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all"
  • 53:7 — "Like a lamb led to the slaughter... he did not open his mouth"
  • 53:8 — "Cut off from the land of the living"
  • 53:9 — "Assigned a grave with the wicked, with the rich in his death"
  • 53:10 — "His life an offering for sin"
  • 53:11 — "After he has suffered, he will see the light of life"
  • 53:12 — "Numbered with the transgressors... bore the sin of many"
Jesus
  • "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him" (John 1:11)
  • Pierced — hands, feet, side
  • "By his wounds you have been healed" (1 Pet 2:24)
  • "For our sake he made him to be sin" (2 Cor 5:21)
  • "He was silent and gave no answer" (Mark 14:61)
  • Killed outside Jerusalem
  • Crucified with criminals; buried in the rich man Joseph of Arimathea's tomb (Matt 27:57–60)
  • "A ransom for many" (Mark 10:45)
  • The resurrection — "he sees light"
  • Crucified between two thieves; "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34)
The mirror of Joseph Notice how Isaiah 53 echoes the story of Joseph: the rejected, suffering one turns out to be the one through whom many are saved. "What you intended for evil, God intended for good, to save many lives" (Gen 50:20). The Christian claim is that this pattern reaches its fullness in Jesus — the brother we did not recognize, who saves us anyway.
vii.
The Pierced One
Zechariah 12:10 · Psalm 16:10 · Daniel 9:26

Three smaller but striking witnesses — pieces of the picture worth knowing about.

Zechariah 12:10 — c. 500 BC
They will look on me, the one they have pierced, and they will mourn for him as one mourns for an only child.
Psalm 16:10 — c. 1000 BC
You will not abandon me to the realm of the dead, nor will you let your faithful one see decay.
Daniel 9:26 — c. 530 BC
After the sixty-two 'sevens,' the Anointed One will be cut off and will have nothing... Most striking is the timing: this prophecy points to a Messiah being "cut off" before the destruction of the Second Temple, which fell in AD 70 — meaning the Messiah had to come before then.
A Closing Blessing · The Road to Emmaus

"Did not our hearts burn within us?"

On the evening Jesus rose, two of his followers walked the road to Emmaus, heartbroken and confused. A stranger joined them. They didn't recognize him. And then — as Luke tells it — he did the very thing this whole study has tried to do.

Luke 24:27, 31–32
And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he explained to them what was said in all the Scriptures concerning himself... Then their eyes were opened and they recognized him... They asked each other, "Were not our hearts burning within us while he talked with us on the road and opened the Scriptures to us?"

That's the hope behind this study. Not to argue you into a conclusion, but to lay the older Scriptures open and let them speak. If Jesus is who Christians say he is, then Moses, the Psalms, and the Prophets were already telling his story long before he arrived — and reading them carefully should be its own kind of evidence.

✦   ✦   ✦

May the same God who opened their eyes on that road open yours.
May the Scriptures speak to you with their own voice.
And may your heart, like theirs, burn with the joy of seeing him.
